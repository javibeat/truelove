7 Signs Your Website Is Costing You Clients

Your site might be quietly turning people away, so here’s how to know and what to do about it.

Here’s an uncomfortable fact about your website: you almost never look at it, and everyone deciding whether to hire you looks at it first. Before they call, before they message, before they walk in, they check. If something feels off, they don’t email you about it. They quietly choose someone else, and you never find out why.

After more than ten years of building and rebuilding websites, I can usually tell within a minute whether a site is helping a business or hurting it. These are the seven checks I run. Do them on your own site today, honestly, and you’ll know where you stand.

1. It’s slow on a phone

Open your site on your phone, on mobile data, not your office wifi. Count. If you’re still looking at a blank screen or a spinner after three seconds, many of your visitors have already gone. People have very little patience for slow pages, and speed is the first impression before any design is even visible. The usual culprits: huge uncompressed photos, heavy themes, and a pile of plugins (add-ons) nobody remembers installing.

2. It falls apart on a small screen

Most of your visitors are probably on a phone. For restaurants, salons, gyms and clinics, it’s nearly all of them. Tiny text you have to zoom into, buttons too small to tap, forms that fight you: each one is a person who gave up. “It technically works on mobile” isn’t the bar. The bar is: can someone standing in the street, holding a coffee, find your prices and contact you in under a minute, with one hand?

Small text and weak contrast don’t only annoy people; they shut some of them out completely. I explain why that matters for your revenue in Accessible Design Is Good Business.

3. Something on it is out of date

Last year’s menu. A “news” section whose latest post is from two years ago. Opening hours that don’t match your door. Prices from before your last increase. A team page with people who left. A property listing that sold months ago. It feels minor, but visitors read it as one loud message: nobody’s home. And if the website is abandoned, they wonder what else is. An outdated site can be worse than no site, because it advertises neglect.

4. A stranger can’t tell what you do in five seconds

Open your homepage and pretend you know nothing about your business. In five seconds, can you answer: what does this company do, for whom, and what should I do next? If the answer is buried under a vague slogan and a stock photo of a handshake, you’re losing the people who were ready to buy. A physiotherapy clinic should say it’s a physiotherapy clinic, where it is and how to book. Clarity beats cleverness every time.

5. It looks like a template, because it is one

This is the 2026 version of an old problem. It has never been easier to get a website: AI builders produce one in minutes. So the internet is now full of sites with the same layouts, the same gradients, the same three boxes of features under the same headline. Visitors have developed an instinct for it. When your site looks interchangeable, your business looks interchangeable, and interchangeable businesses end up competing on price alone. I wrote more about that trap in Web Design in the Age of AI.

6. Contacting you takes effort

Count the taps between landing on your site and actually reaching you. If your phone number isn’t tappable, if the contact form has nine fields, if WhatsApp, where so many of your customers already are, is nowhere to be found, you’re making interested people work to give you money. Some will. Most won’t.

The same goes for a hotel without a clear booking button, a consultant without a simple way to request a call, or an online shop that hides delivery costs until the last step. Every extra step loses people.

7. You don’t show up when people ask AI

Not long ago this was new. Now it’s normal. Many people ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude or Google’s AI Overviews (the summary at the top of search results) for recommendations instead of scrolling through links: “best paediatric dentist in Dubai Marina”, “who can build a website for my restaurant”. These tools can only recommend what they can read and understand. If your site is mostly images, with little real text, no clear structure and nothing that explains what you do and where, you’re easy to leave out of those answers.

Try it: ask an AI assistant about your type of business in your city. If you’re not mentioned, future clients are being sent somewhere else. I explain what to fix in How to Get Found When People Ask AI Instead of Google.

Scored yourself? Here’s what it means

Zero or one sign: your site is doing its job. Keep it updated and move on.

Two or three: you probably don’t need a rebuild. You need targeted fixes.

Four or more: your website is working against you, and every month of “we’ll deal with it later” costs you clients who never called.

One honest warning from someone in the business: the answer isn’t automatically “pay for a big redesign.” Sometimes it is. Sometimes it’s three small fixes. If you’re not sure which camp you’re in, that’s exactly the question to ask a professional before spending anything. A short conversation usually settles it, and you’ll know whether you’re looking at a small repair or a full rebuild.

Accessible Design Is Good Business

Designing for people who find the web harder makes your website better for every customer, and easier for search and AI to read.

When people hear “accessibility”, they often picture a compliance checklist, something for big corporations and government websites. I understand why. But after years of building sites for all kinds of businesses, I see it differently. Accessible design is simply design that works for more people, in more situations. And that turns out to be very good for business.

Think about who actually visits your website. Someone reading on their phone in bright sunlight. Someone whose eyesight isn’t what it was. Someone with a broken wrist using only the keyboard. Someone watching your video on a train with the sound off. Someone who gets dizzy when everything on the screen slides around. None of them would describe themselves as “a user with accessibility needs”. They’re just your customers, and right now some of them might be struggling.

What accessible design actually means

There is an international standard for this: the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, usually called WCAG, published by the W3C, the body that looks after web standards. It’s technical, but the ideas behind it are common sense. People should be able to see your content, understand it, move around it whichever way they use a device, and rely on it working with the tools they use, like screen readers (software that reads the page aloud).

You don’t need to read the standard. You need to know enough to ask your designer the right questions. So here are the areas that matter most, in plain language.

The areas that make the biggest difference

Contrast you can actually read

Light grey text on a white background looks elegant in a presentation and becomes unreadable on a phone outdoors. Good contrast between text and background is the cheapest accessibility win there is, and it helps literally everyone. WCAG sets minimum contrast levels, and a good designer checks them as a habit, not as an afterthought.

Type that’s comfortable to read

Body text big enough to read without zooming, line lengths that don’t stretch across the whole screen, enough space between lines. And the site should still work if someone makes the text bigger in their phone settings. A restaurant menu or a clinic’s list of services that breaks when the text is enlarged is losing exactly the people who most need to read it.

Everything works with a keyboard

Some people never use a mouse or a touchscreen. They move through a page with the Tab key or with other assistive devices. Every menu, button, form and pop-up should work that way, and you should always be able to see where you are on the page. Try it yourself: open your website, put the mouse aside and press Tab. If you get lost, so do they.

Screen readers can make sense of it

People who are blind or have low vision often use a screen reader. For it to work, the page needs real structure: proper headings, buttons that are actually buttons, links that say where they go (“See our prices” rather than “Click here”), and images with short text descriptions, called alt text. This is the same structure that helps search engines, as you’ll see in a moment.

Captions and transcripts

If you use video, add captions. They help people who are deaf or hard of hearing, and also the many people who watch with the sound off. A short transcript for a podcast or an interview does the same for audio, and gives search engines text they can read.

Dark mode, done properly

Many people set their phone to dark mode to reduce glare, save battery or simply because it’s easier on their eyes at night. A site that respects that setting feels considerate. Doing it well means more than inverting colours: contrast still has to hold, and logos and photos still have to look right.

Respecting motion sensitivity

Big parallax effects and things flying in from every side can cause discomfort or even nausea for some people. Phones and computers have a “reduce motion” setting, and a well-built site listens to it, toning down animations for those who asked. Movement should support the content, never get in the way of it.

Forms that don’t fight you

Forms are where businesses earn their money: bookings, enquiries, checkouts, sign-ups. Every field should have a visible label (not just grey placeholder text that disappears when you start typing). Errors should explain what went wrong and how to fix it, in words, not only in red. Don’t ask for more than you need. A gym membership form or a property enquiry that’s easy to complete is simply a form that converts better.

Why it’s good business, not just good manners

It helps every customer. Clear contrast, readable text and simple forms make a site easier for people in a hurry, people on small screens and people using a second language. Accessibility improvements are usability improvements.

It helps search engines and AI assistants read you. Search engines and AI tools like ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google’s AI Overviews read your website a bit like a screen reader does: through its structure and text. Clear headings, descriptive links, alt text on images and transcripts on videos give them more to understand and quote. I wrote more about this in how to get found when people ask AI instead of Google.

It reflects on your brand. A hotel, a school or a clinic that’s easy to use for everyone sends a quiet message: we thought about you. A site that shuts people out sends the opposite one, and most of them won’t tell you. They just leave, which is one of the signs I listed in 7 signs your website is costing you clients.

It’s cheaper to build in than to bolt on. Getting the structure, colours and forms right from the first design costs very little extra. Fixing them on a finished site means redoing work you’ve already paid for.

A note on rules: in many countries accessibility is also a legal expectation for some kinds of organisations, and the details vary by country and sector. I’m a designer, not a lawyer, so if that applies to you, check with someone qualified. But even where nobody requires it, it’s worth doing for the reasons above.

“Design for the person having the hardest time, and you end up with something easier for everyone.”

A checklist to bring to your designer

You don’t need to be technical to ask these. A good designer or studio should be happy to answer them clearly:

  • Do you check colour contrast against WCAG as you design?
  • Does the site still work if I make the text bigger on my phone?
  • Can someone use the whole site with only a keyboard, and can they always see where they are?
  • Are you using proper headings, buttons and link text, so screen readers can follow the page?
  • Who writes the alt text for images, and will I be able to add it myself when I upload new ones?
  • Will videos have captions? Can I add them to future videos easily?
  • Will the site support dark mode, and how will you keep contrast right in both?
  • Does the site respect the “reduce motion” setting?
  • Do all form fields have visible labels and clear error messages?
  • How will you test it: with real keyboards, screen readers and phones, not only automated tools?

If the answer to most of these is a confident yes with an explanation, you’re in good hands. If the answer is “we can add that later”, ask how, and what it will cost.

How to Write a Website Brief That Gets You an Accurate Quote

What to prepare before you contact a designer, the mistakes that cost you time, and a shortcut that takes three minutes.

Most of the quotes that go wrong go wrong before anyone opens a design file. A client writes “we need a new website, how much?”, the studio guesses, and two people walk away with completely different pictures in their heads. One imagined five pages and a contact form. The other imagined an online shop with bookings, three languages and a members area. Neither was lying. Nobody wrote anything down.

I’ve read a lot of briefs since 2015, from one-line WhatsApp messages to forty-page PDFs. The best ones are rarely the longest. They’re the ones that answer a handful of questions honestly. Here’s what those questions are, what to prepare before you contact any designer or studio, and the mistakes I see most often.

Why the brief matters more than you think

A brief does two jobs. It lets the studio give you a quote that reflects the real work, and it gives the designer something to design towards. Without it, a quote is a guess with a number on it, and the design becomes a series of “not quite” rounds that cost both sides time and patience.

A good brief doesn’t need jargon. You don’t have to know what a CMS is or how hosting works. You just need to explain your business, your customers and what success looks like. The technical translation is our job.

What to prepare before you contact anyone

1. The goal, in one sentence

Not “a modern website”. What should the website do? Get more table bookings for a restaurant. Bring in enquiries for a property agency. Sell skincare online. Make a clinic look as trustworthy online as it is in person. Help a startup look credible in front of investors.

If you have more than one goal, put them in order. “More bookings first, then show the new menu” leads to a very different homepage than the other way round.

2. Who it’s for

Describe your ideal customer as if you were telling a friend. “Busy parents in Dubai looking for after-school activities.” “Facility managers at mid-sized companies.” “Tourists deciding where to have dinner tonight, on their phone, in the street.” The more specific you are, the better the design decisions get: tone, layout, what goes first, what can wait.

3. Examples you like, and why

This is the most useful thing you can send, and the most often half-done. Two or three links to websites you like are great. But the reason matters more than the link. “I like this hotel site because the photos are huge and it feels calm” tells me something. “I like this one” tells me almost nothing, because I might love it for completely different reasons.

Examples you dislike help just as much. “Nothing that looks like a bank” rules out a whole direction in one sentence.

4. What content you already have

Do you have a logo? Brand colours? Professional photos? Written text for each page? A domain name? Be honest here, because content is where projects quietly stall. A gym with great photos and finished copy is a very different project from a gym with a logo and a plan to “write something soon”. If you need help with copy or photography, say so up front so it’s part of the plan and the quote, not a surprise in the middle.

5. The must-haves, separated from the nice-to-haves

Make two short lists. Must-haves are the things without which the site fails: online payments for a shop, a booking calendar for a salon, property listings you can update yourself, Arabic and English for a school. Nice-to-haves are the things you’d like if the budget allows: a blog, a newsletter, a live chat.

This split is gold for a studio. It lets us show you what the core project looks like and what each extra adds, instead of one big number you can’t pull apart.

One must-have that rarely makes the list but should: a site that everyone can use, whatever their eyesight or device. I explain why in accessible design is good business.

6. An honest budget

I understand why people keep their budget secret. It feels like showing your cards. But a website can be a focused one-page site or a full platform, and both are “a website”. If you tell me roughly what you planned to spend, I can tell you honestly what that buys, what I’d prioritise and where I’d hold back. If it isn’t enough for what you want, I’d rather say it on day one than after three meetings.

A range is fine. “Somewhere between X and Y” is enough to start a real conversation.

7. Timing, and why

Is there a real deadline, like a launch, an opening day or an event? Or is it “as soon as possible” because you’re tired of the old site? Both are fine, but they’re not the same. A fixed date shapes the plan. At True Love, timing depends on the design and the scope, so we agree it together after the first conversation rather than promising a number of weeks before we know the project.

The mistakes I see most often

  • Describing the solution instead of the problem. “We need a chatbot” is a solution. “People keep asking the same five questions on WhatsApp” is the problem, and there might be a simpler answer.
  • Leaving out the awkward history. If a previous agency let you down, tell me what happened. It helps me avoid repeating it.
  • Asking for “something like Apple”. Fine as a feeling. But Apple’s site works for Apple’s products and audience. Tell me what you admire about it, and I’ll work out what applies to a dental clinic.
  • Forgetting who updates the site. If you want to change prices, menus or listings yourself every week, that changes how the site is built. Mention it.
  • Hiding the budget and then rejecting every proposal on price. Everyone loses a week.
  • Sending it to ten studios at once with no detail. You’ll get ten quotes for ten different projects and no way to compare them.

A brief you can copy

If you prefer to write your own, this structure covers almost everything:

  1. What the business does, in two sentences.
  2. The main goal of the website (and the second one, if there is one).
  3. Who the ideal customer is.
  4. Two or three sites you like, and what you like about each. One you don’t, and why.
  5. The content you have and the content you need help with.
  6. Must-have features. Nice-to-have features.
  7. Rough budget range.
  8. Any real deadline.
  9. Anything else: past experiences, competitors, worries.
“A clear brief doesn’t limit the designer. It gives them something worth aiming at.”

Or let the form ask you the questions

Because most people don’t enjoy writing briefs, we built one that does the asking. Our project brief walks you through it in about three minutes. You pick what you’re building (a website, an app or both), the type of site and the features you need, plus extras like a logo if you want them. Then it asks what the project should achieve, who it’s for, the style you’re after with room for reference websites, what content you already have, how soon you need it, and anything else worth knowing.

As you choose, it shows a live estimate in dirhams, euros, pounds or dollars. It’s approximate by design: it gives you a realistic starting point, and we adjust it to your real needs after our first conversation. If you’ve already got an idea of your budget, the last step is a good place to mention it.

Whether you use the form or write your own, the point is the same: the clearer you are at the start, the more accurate the quote and the better the result.

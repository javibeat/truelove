Why Every DJ, Musician and Performer Needs a Professional Website

Instagram is not your website, and for anyone who gets booked as a person, that matters.

This might sting a little. You have thousands of followers, your mixes get shared, you play good venues. And when someone searches your name, what comes up? An Instagram profile, maybe a SoundCloud page. That’s your whole online identity, and you don’t own any of it.

I know this because I’m a DJ myself, playing in Dubai. Over the years I’ve also built websites for musicians like Kiko Navarro, Manuel KevSax, Sergio Trumpet and Julio Cuba. I’ve seen what changes when an artist gets a proper site.

This post talks mostly about DJs and musicians, because that’s where I see it most. But it applies just as much to anyone who is booked or hired as a person: singers, dancers, magicians, hosts and MCs, photographers, speakers, coaches, make-up artists, chefs who do private events. If people book you, read on.

Instagram is rented land

Instagram, TikTok, SoundCloud: none of them are yours. The algorithm changes and your reach drops overnight. An account gets hacked or flagged and years of posts disappear. It happens, including to people with very large followings.

Your website is your land. You decide what goes on it, how it looks and what people see first. No algorithm choosing whether your work gets shown. No competing with memes for attention. Someone types your name, and they find you.

Social media is still where people discover you. Keep it. Just make sure it points somewhere you control.

The booking question

Here’s how it plays out. A hotel events manager is putting together a season of live music and needs a saxophonist. Someone recommends a name. The manager searches it.

Scenario A: they find an Instagram profile. They scroll, watch a reel, can’t find booking details. They send a DM. Maybe it’s seen, maybe not. They move on to the next name.

Scenario B: they find a site like Manuel KevSax’s. Professional photos, video, a clear list of past venues and a booking form. They fill it in in two minutes.

Which one do you think gets the call back first?

The same scene happens with a wedding planner looking for a violinist, a brand looking for a photographer like Estrela Photo, or someone choosing a wellness coach like Jessica Morari. The people who make hiring decisions want to check you quickly, in one place, and feel sure.

AI is now part of the booking

In late 2026, that events manager might not even search. They might ask ChatGPT or another assistant: “recommend a live saxophonist in Dubai for a hotel brunch.” Assistants answer by reading websites. An Instagram grid gives them very little to go on. A clear website with your name, what you do, where you perform and who you’ve played for gives them something to quote.

I explain how this works in how to get found when people ask AI instead of Google. The short version: if you only exist on social media, you’re much harder to recommend.

What I’ve seen happen

When I built Kiko Navarro’s website, the goal wasn’t just a nice page. Kiko has been producing and performing for decades, and his online presence didn’t reflect that. Now, when a promoter, a label or a journalist looks him up, they find a site that matches who he is. That changes how people approach an artist.

I’ve seen the same with other performers I’ve worked with. A proper site moves the first message from “how much for a gig?” towards “we’d love to work with you, what’s your availability?” It doesn’t replace talent or reputation. It makes them visible.

What a performer’s website actually needs

It doesn’t need to be complicated. A good site for a DJ, musician or any performer has:

  • A strong visual identity. Your brand, not a generic template. The site should feel the way your work feels. If you don’t have an identity yet, start with branding.
  • A bio and press kit. A short bio, a longer one, high-resolution photos people can download, and your key highlights. Make life easy for whoever is booking you.
  • Your work, front and centre. Embedded mixes and tracks, live clips, a photo gallery, a showreel. Let the work speak.
  • A booking form. Not a DM, not “link in bio”. A real form that asks for the date, the venue and the type of event, so you get useful enquiries.
  • Upcoming dates and past venues. If you’re playing or appearing somewhere, show it. Proof matters in this business.
  • Clear, readable text. Who you are, what you do and where you work, written in plain words, so both people and AI assistants understand you.

Unlike a single gig, a website keeps working when you’re not. It’s there when the promoter looks you up at 2am, and when someone in another country hears your mix and wants to know more.

I’ve been on both sides of this, as a DJ who needed a site and as the person who builds them. It’s one of the few things in this career that keeps working for you long after it’s done.

“Your website is your stage when you’re not on stage.”

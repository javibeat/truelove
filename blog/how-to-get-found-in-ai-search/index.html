How to Get Found When People Ask AI Instead of Google

Your next client might never see a results page, so here is how to stay visible when an AI does the recommending.

Ask a few people how they found the last restaurant they booked, the last clinic they visited or the last accountant they hired. A few years ago the answer was always “I googled it.” Now you hear “I asked ChatGPT” just as often. Or Perplexity, or Claude, or the assistant built into their phone.

Even the people who still google don’t see what they used to. Google’s AI Overviews answer the question at the top of the page, before a single website link. Plenty of searches now end without anyone clicking anything. The industry calls it zero-click search. I call it the biggest change to being found online since Google itself.

By late 2026 this isn’t a trend to watch. It’s simply how a large part of your future clients look for things. So the question for any business owner is practical: when someone asks an AI about what you do, does your name come up?

What actually changed

The old game was simple. Rank on page one, get the click, make your case on your own website. The new game has an extra player in the middle. An AI reads a lot of websites, forms an answer and recommends. Your future client may hear about you, or not, from a machine that read your website on their behalf.

That has a hard consequence: if AI can’t read your site, you don’t exist in that conversation. Many sites, especially the ones thrown together with page builders, are genuinely hard for these systems to understand. Text locked inside images. No clear structure. Vague copy that never says plainly what the business does, where it works or roughly what it costs.

Think of a dental clinic in Jumeirah whose homepage says “Your smile, our passion” and nothing else above the fold. A person might scroll and work it out. An assistant asked “good family dentist near Jumeirah that’s open on Saturdays” has nothing to quote.

The good news

What surprised me when I started working on this for clients is how little of it is new. Almost everything that works is honest, old-fashioned clarity. AI assistants recommend businesses they can understand and check. So the checklist looks like this.

1. Say what you do in plain words

“Family-run Italian restaurant in Dubai Marina, open since 2018, with a terrace and a set lunch on weekdays” beats “a culinary journey like no other” every time. An AI can quote the first sentence. It can do nothing with the second. The same goes for a gym, a law firm, a property agent or a software startup.

2. Answer real questions on your site

When someone asks an assistant “how much does a website cost in Dubai?” or “does this hotel allow pets?”, it looks for pages that answer exactly that. Publish your prices or price ranges, your process, your opening hours, your FAQ. That’s why I wrote an honest piece on what a website costs in Dubai instead of hiding behind “contact us for a quote.” Businesses that hide information become invisible.

3. Add structured data

Structured data is a machine-readable label inside your site: your name, address, services, opening hours, prices and reviews, written in a format search engines and assistants trust. Your visitors never see it. For machines, it’s often the clearest thing on the page.

4. Keep pages fast, clean and readable

AI crawlers give up on slow, bloated pages even faster than people do. A site that is built properly, with real headings, real text and clean code, is readable by everything. That’s the whole point of building it by hand rather than stacking plugins.

5. Make it accessible

The things that help a screen reader help an AI too: proper headings, descriptive links, text alternatives for images. I wrote more about this in why accessible design is good business. It’s the rare piece of work that pays off twice.

6. Be mentioned elsewhere

Assistants cross-check. Reviews on Google, listings in the directories that matter for your industry, press, partners and clients linking to you. If the only place you exist is your own website, you’re a rumour, not a fact.

“AI search rewards the businesses with nothing to hide and quietly ignores the ones hiding behind buzzwords.”

What I do on every site I build

Every website that leaves the studio ships with the basics done properly: structured data that describes the business, semantic markup, a sitemap, fast load times, and copy written to answer the questions clients actually ask. Some of it, like an llms.txt file (a plain summary written specifically for AI crawlers), barely existed a few years ago. This site has one.

None of this is magic, and be wary of anyone selling it as magic. There is no secret trick that makes ChatGPT recommend you, and nobody can guarantee you’ll appear in a particular answer. There is only being findable, readable and worth recommending. Many businesses in Dubai still haven’t covered the basics. That’s the opportunity.

A quick self-check

Try this today. It takes ten minutes.

  1. Ask two or three AI assistants a question your ideal client would ask, the way they’d ask it. “Best physiotherapy clinic in Al Barsha for sports injuries.” “Who designs restaurant menus in Dubai?”
  2. See who gets named. If it isn’t you, open the sites that were named and compare them with yours.
  3. Read your own homepage as if you were a stranger. Can you tell, in one sentence, what you do, where and for whom?
  4. Check whether your prices, hours and contact details are written as text, not hidden in a PDF or an image.

If that last check made you wince, my list of signs your website is costing you clients is a good next read.

The part nobody wants to hear

Visibility gets you into the conversation. It doesn’t win it. When an assistant gives someone three options and they open yours, you have a few seconds of human attention. Then it’s back to the oldest question there is: does this site make me trust these people?

That’s design, and it’s the part no machine does for you. Getting found is the new front door. People still buy from people.

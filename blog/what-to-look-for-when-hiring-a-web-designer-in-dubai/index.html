What to Look for When Hiring a Web Designer in Dubai

Red flags, smart questions and how to tell good from great before you sign anything.

You’ve decided you need a website. You’ve searched “web designer Dubai”, or asked an AI assistant, and now you’re drowning in options. Agencies, freelancers, “full-service digital solutions”. Beautiful portfolios, big claims, five-star reviews that all sound strangely alike. And you’re thinking: how do I actually tell who’s good?

I’ve been designing and building websites in Dubai since 2015, and I’ve been on both sides of the table: hiring creatives and being hired as one. This is the guide I wish someone had handed me the first time I hired a designer. It works whether you run a restaurant, a clinic, a property agency, an online shop or a startup that hasn’t launched yet.

The Dubai problem

Dubai has too much of everything. You’ll find agencies with glass offices and large teams quoting AED 50,000 or more. You’ll find marketplace freelancers charging AED 500 for a template with your logo dropped in. And in late 2026 you’ll also find people selling sites generated by AI builders in an afternoon, which look exactly like everybody else’s.

Everyone says “award-winning” and “results-driven”. Every portfolio looks great, because portfolios are curated. The real question isn’t who looks good on paper. It’s who actually delivers. Here’s how to find out.

Look at live sites, not mockups

If a designer only shows you Figma screens, Behance shots or PDFs, be careful. A mockup is a promise. A live website is proof.

Ask for links. Open them on your phone. Check how fast they load. Find the contact page. Fill in a form. If a designer can’t point you to real, working websites, that tells you a lot.

When someone asks me for examples, I send them to live projects like Jessica Morari, a coaching and wellness brand, or Estrela Photo, a photography portfolio. Real sites, live right now. Click through, resize the window, test the speed. That’s the standard I hold myself to, and it’s the one you should hold any designer to. You can see the rest in the work section.

Ask who actually does the work

This catches people off guard. You meet an agency. The person across the table is polished and says all the right things. You sign. Then you never hear from that person again, and your project is handed to a team you’ve never met.

Remote teams aren’t the problem. Some of the best developers I know work from other continents. But you should know who is designing and building your site, and you should be able to talk to them. When the person selling the project and the people doing it never overlap, things get lost.

With True Love, you work with me directly. I’m the one who designs it, who builds it, and who answers your message when something needs fixing before launch.

Ask how they use AI

This is the new question, and a fair one. Almost every studio uses AI tools now, me included. That’s fine. What matters is whether AI is helping skilled people work better, or replacing the thinking you’re paying for.

Ask them straight: what do you use it for, and what do you never hand over to it? A good answer is specific. A vague or defensive one is a warning. I wrote about this in more depth in web design in the age of AI.

Check the tech, not just the design

A beautiful website that takes eight seconds to load is not doing its job. Visitors leave before they read a word, and search engines notice.

Ask about speed. Ask about mobile. Ask about SEO, the real kind. Can they explain what structured data is? Do they know what a Lighthouse score measures? And now: what do they do so that AI assistants can read and recommend the site? If that last question gets a blank look, read how to get found in AI search before you sign anything.

A simple test: run one of their live sites through Google PageSpeed Insights. A low score on mobile means they aren’t paying attention to what matters. On my own sites, I treat a low score as a bug to fix, not a detail.

Read the contract before you pay

I’ve heard the horror stories from clients who came to me afterwards. A business that wanted to change hosting and found the agency “owned” the code. “Unlimited revisions” that turned out to mean three rounds, buried in the small print. A monthly maintenance fee for a site that didn’t need maintaining.

Before you sign, make sure you know:

  • How many revisions are included, and what counts as one.
  • Who owns the code, the design and the domain when it’s finished.
  • What happens after launch: is there a handover, and what support is included?
  • What the payment schedule looks like: deposit, milestones, final payment.
  • Whether there are any recurring fees you didn’t expect.

A good designer is upfront about all of this before you even ask. If they’re vague about money, they’ll be vague about everything else.

Be wary of fixed timelines before anyone understands your project

Some studios promise “your site in two weeks” before they know anything about your business. Speed isn’t bad, but a timeline given before the first real conversation is a sales line, not a plan. How long a site takes depends on the design, the scope and how ready your content is. A serious designer agrees the timing with you once they understand what you need.

The longevity test

Don’t stop at screenshots. Visit the actual sites from their portfolio, including older ones.

Are they still live? Do they still look good? Are they still fast? A designer with five great sites that are still running years later is worth more than one with fifty that are broken, offline or frozen in 2019. If most of someone’s past work has disappeared, ask why.

Price is not quality

I wrote a whole article about how much a website costs in Dubai, so I won’t repeat it here. The short version: expensive doesn’t automatically mean good, and very cheap almost always means corners were cut.

A large agency quote often pays for offices, sales teams and account managers as much as for your website. The AED 500 option usually gives you a template and hope. What you want is someone with real experience and a portfolio of live work, who isn’t carrying heavy overhead, and who is honest enough to tell you what you actually need rather than what makes the invoice bigger.

Trust your gut

If communication is poor before you’ve paid, it will be worse after. If they take five days to reply while they’re trying to win you, imagine waiting for an urgent fix on your live site.

Notice how they talk to you. Do they ask about your business, or only talk about themselves? Do they listen? Do they explain things clearly, or hide behind jargon? A website project is a working relationship. You should enjoy it.

I’ve turned down projects when the fit wasn’t right, and I’d rather a client told me honestly we weren’t a match than have us both pretend.

“A good designer doesn’t just build what you ask for. They tell you what you actually need.”

Red flags checklist

  • Only mockups, no live links
  • Won’t say who designs and builds the site
  • No mention of speed, SEO or AI visibility
  • Vague contract or surprise fees
  • A fixed timeline before they understand your project
  • Slow replies before you’ve paid
  • Portfolio sites that are offline or broken
  • Can’t explain how they use AI, or what they use to build

Frequently asked

How do I check if a web designer is good?

Ask for live links, not mockups. Open their sites on your phone. Run them through Google PageSpeed Insights. Check that older projects are still live and working. That tells you more than any pitch.

Is it better to hire an agency or a freelancer in Dubai?

Neither is automatically better. Agencies carry overhead that pushes prices up. The cheapest freelancers cut corners. Often the best option is an experienced independent studio where you deal directly with the person doing the work.

What should a web design contract include?

Revisions, ownership of code, design and domain, the payment schedule, what support you get after launch, and any recurring fees. If any of these are vague, ask before you sign.

How long does it take to build a website?

It depends on the design and the scope: a single landing page and an online shop with bookings are very different projects. A good designer agrees the timing with you after the first conversation, not before.

What should I prepare before talking to a designer?

A short description of your business, who you want to reach, a few sites you like and why, and a rough budget. My guide on how to write a website brief walks you through it.
